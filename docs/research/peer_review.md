# Peer Review and Referee Training

## Overview

Peer review is an important part of the research process and a valuable professional development opportunity. Reviewing papers helps researchers improve their critical reading skills, learn how journals evaluate manuscripts, and gain exposure to new research before publication.

While these opportunities are likely to be most valuable for predocs and PhD students who are learning the review process, they may also be useful for researchers seeking exposure to a new field, literature, or journal audience. For example, researchers transitioning between disciplines may find it helpful to compare how manuscripts are evaluated across different academic communities.

ECHOLab therefore encourages researchers interested in peer review to express interest when opportunities arise.

## Why Learn to Review?

Participating in peer review can help researchers:

* Develop critical reading skills
* Learn how journals evaluate manuscripts
* Better understand standards of evidence and causal inference
* Improve their own academic writing
* Anticipate concerns that reviewers may raise about their work
* Gain exposure to new research before publication

Many researchers find that reviewing papers improves their own research as much as it helps the broader scientific community.

## Opportunities to Co-Review

From time to time, Sam receives requests to review manuscripts from academic journals.

Researchers who are interested in learning more about the peer review process are encouraged to express interest in participating as co-reviewers.

A typical co-review experience may involve:

1. Reading the manuscript independently.
2. Writing an independent referee report and recommendation.
3. Comparing assessments and discussing strengths and weaknesses of the paper.
4. Reviewing the final report submitted to the journal.
5. Comparing evaluations across referees when those reviews become available.

Potential topics of discussion include:

* Research design and identification
* Data quality and measurement
* Statistical methods
* Interpretation of results
* Framing and contribution
* Clarity of presentation
* Appropriate recommendations to the editor

The goal is not simply to contribute to a review, but to learn how experienced researchers evaluate manuscripts and how editorial decisions are made.

Many researchers find that writing independent reviews and comparing them to those of other referees is one of the fastest ways to improve both reviewing skills and their own research.

Participation is entirely optional and opportunities will depend on manuscript topic, timing, workload, and journal policies.

## Journal Policies and Confidentiality

Opportunities to participate in co-reviewing depend in part on journal policies. In some fields, particularly health and interdisciplinary sciences, journals often explicitly permit or encourage involvement of junior researchers. In other fields, policies may be more restrictive.

In all cases, participation will be conducted in accordance with the journal's policies and the expectations of the handling editor.

Journal manuscripts under review are confidential.

Researchers should not share manuscripts, discuss them outside the review process, or retain copies after the review has been completed unless permitted by journal policy.

## Recognition

Many journals allow invited reviewers to acknowledge co-reviewers. Policies vary by journal.

When permitted by the journal, co-reviewers may be credited for their contributions.

## Expressing Interest

Researchers interested in learning more about peer review, gaining experience as a reviewer, or obtaining exposure to a new literature are encouraged to let Sam know.

Interest does not create any obligation to participate, but it can help identify researchers who would like to be considered when suitable opportunities arise.
